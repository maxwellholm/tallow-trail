What are seed oils, and why avoid them?

"Seed oils" usually refers to a group of industrially refined oils extracted from seeds rather than pressed from fruit — canola, soybean, corn, sunflower, safflower, grapeseed, cottonseed, and rice bran oil are the most common. They became the default cooking fat in most restaurants and packaged food starting in the mid-20th century because they're cheap, shelf-stable, and neutral-tasting.

A growing number of people are choosing to avoid them, pointing to their high concentration of polyunsaturated fats and the heavy processing involved, and preferring traditional fats instead — tallow, lard, butter, ghee, coconut oil, and cold-pressed olive or avocado oil. This remains a debated area of nutrition science: mainstream dietary guidelines still generally favor unsaturated fats over saturated ones, while critics argue the research hasn't kept pace with how much seed oil consumption has changed over the past century.

Whatever your reasoning, actually avoiding them at restaurants is hard — most kitchens use them by default for frying and sautéing, and it's rarely listed on a menu. That's what this page tracks: which restaurants use something else, and how to order around the ones that don't.
